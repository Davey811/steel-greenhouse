What the switch changes

Everything else is identical: the sphere receives the core's power plus whatever the shell radiates onto it, and emits σT⁴ from its surface; the shell radiates to space from its outer face.

Inner face absorbs only is the boundary condition in Postma's simulation. The shell soaks up the sphere's radiation, and in his "back-radiation" run the sphere also receives A_sp·σ·T_sh⁴ from the shell, but the shell never loses it. Energy is counted twice, so the ledger fills with energy created from nothing: in his own figure, "energy created = P_back". The temperature rise in that run is real arithmetic on a broken ledger.

Both faces emit is ordinary physics. A surface that absorbs infrared also emits it (Kirchhoff's law, which follows from the second law of thermodynamics). What the sphere receives from the shell, the shell loses. The ledger stays at zero, space receives exactly the core's power once the system settles, and the sphere still ends up warmer than without the shell: σT_sp⁴ = 235 × (1 + (r_sp/r_sh)²) W/m² for a black shell. With Postma's geometry: 277.6 K instead of 253.7 K.

Net heat always flows from the warmer sphere to the cooler shell. The shell does not heat the sphere; the core does. The shell reduces how fast the sphere loses heat, so the sphere must get warmer to get rid of the same 2953 W.

The same thing keeps spacecraft instruments warm: a radioisotope heater of about 1 W, wrapped in multilayer insulation, in vacuum.

Lumped model (one temperature for the sphere, one for the shell; the steel wall's conduction drop is 0.05 K). The Python script in this repository solves the radial heat-conduction equation through the wall and reproduces Postma's figures number by number. Sphere C = 0.95 MJ/K; shell: steel, 2 cm thick, ρ = 7850 kg/m³, c = 490 J/(kg·K).
